# Complaint escalation

A complaint is won on dates, references and a clear ask, not on how angry it sounds. The letters here are
short and firm, the escalation route is named by category rather than by country, and the contact log is
what the user can point at when a company changes its story. Nothing is ever sent, no email address is ever
entered, and no legal right is claimed that has not been verified.

## When to use

- "They won't refund me." / "I've asked three times and they keep saying no."
- "Write a complaint letter" — to a shop, a supplier, a utility, an airline, a platform.
- "This company is ignoring me" — support tickets closed with no answer, no reply for weeks.
- "Escalate this" — a first request went nowhere and they want the next level.
- A faulty product, a service that was never delivered, a charge for something cancelled, a repair promised and not done.
- They want a paper trail before asking their bank to reverse a card payment.
- A complaint has a deadline attached (a notice period, a return window, a dispute window) and the clock is running.

## When not to use

- **A dispute with a private individual** — a neighbour, a friend, a marketplace seller who is a person, someone who owes them money. There is no company to escalate to and no consumer body; that is a difficult-message problem, and a formal letter usually makes it worse.
- **Tenancy, employment, immigration, benefits, medical or family matters.** Not consumer transactions. These need a professional, not this procedure.
- **Understanding a letter they received** — a rejection, a final position, a settlement offer. Go to `explain-this-document` first; come back here to reply.
- **A billing error you can simply show in a table** — a duplicate charge, a price rise, a forgotten subscription. That is `bill-triage`.
- **Producing the form that starts a formal claim** — go to `paperwork-forms`.
- **Just softening or proofreading a letter they already wrote** — that is editing, not this procedure.
- **Deciding whether they have a legal case, or how much they are entitled to.** This skill never answers that.

## Procedure

1. **Build the fact sheet before writing a word.** Record: what was bought or promised, the date, the amount and currency, the order or reference number, what went wrong, every contact so far (date, channel, who, what they said, what they promised), the remedy wanted, and the evidence in hand (receipt, photos, emails, screenshots, delivery tracking). Anything unknown goes in a "needed" list — never a plausible-looking date or amount.

   **Done when:** the fact sheet is written and every field is either filled from the user's own material or marked "unknown — needed".

2. **Settle the remedy in one sentence.** Primary ask and a fallback, both concrete and time-bound: "full refund of 120 by [date]" or "repair within 14 days, otherwise a replacement". If the user is unsure, lay out two or three realistic outcomes with what each costs them to pursue, and let them pick.

   **Done when:** the remedy is one sentence containing a specific action and a number or a date, and the user has confirmed it.

3. **Fix the timeline and the boundary of the law.** Note any deadline the company set, any return or cooling-off window the user mentions, and any dispute window their card issuer has. Then state plainly that you have not verified their local consumer law and that a local consumer advice service can confirm what it gives them. Do not assert a right, an entitlement, or a statutory period as fact.

   **Done when:** the letter has a real reply date and the file contains a plain line saying local law has not been verified.

4. **Write the first letter.** Structure, in this order: their reference and what the purchase was; what happened, as dated facts with no adjectives; what has already been tried, with dates; the remedy wanted and the deadline for a reply; what happens next if there is no reply; how to reach the user. Short — one page. Firm, not abusive.

   **Done when:** the letter contains the reference, the dated facts, the remedy, an explicit reply date, the stated next step, and no claim of a legal right that was not verified.

5. **Name the escalation route by category, not by country.** Identify the kind of body that handles this sector in general terms — a consumer-protection agency, a sector regulator, an ombudsman or alternative-dispute-resolution scheme, or the dispute scheme named inside the contract. Then tell the user how to find the actual one where they live, and to confirm it before relying on it. Never name a specific national regulator as if it were theirs.

   **Done when:** the file names the category that fits this sector and gives the user a way to confirm their local body, with no specific national regulator stated as fact.

6. **Draft the escalation letter — only when it is earned.** Two conditions: the first deadline passed with no reply, or the company refused. Restate the facts in one short paragraph, reference the first letter by date, state the response received or the silence, state the outcome sought, list the evidence enclosed, and give a final date.

   **Done when:** the escalation draft cites the first letter's date, states the company's reply or its absence, and lists the attachments.

7. **Keep the contact log.** Table: date | who | channel | what was said | what they promised | next action and by when. Fill it from the user's account of events and keep it current — it is the document that settles arguments later. Add the next expected event as an open row.

   **Done when:** the log has one row per contact the user described, plus one open row for the next expected reply.

8. **Hand over with a sending plan.** Which letter to send, to what kind of address (the complaints address on the contract or receipt, not a general support inbox), what to attach, how to keep proof of sending, and when to come back if the deadline passes. Say plainly that nothing has been sent.

   **Done when:** the chat reply names the file to send, the recipient type, the attachments, the reply date, and the words that nothing was sent.

## Gotchas

- Never send, never fill in a web form on the user's behalf, never enter an email address anywhere. The user sends from their own address so the reply comes to them.
- A threat the user will not carry out makes the letter weaker, not stronger. Only write "I will escalate to X" when that is true.
- Give the deadline as both a number and a date — "14 days, by 3 March" — because "within 14 days" gets argued about.
- Keep adjectives out of the facts. "Disgusting service" invites a defensive reply; "the item arrived broken on 2 May" does not.
- One case per letter. Three complaints in one letter get one answer, usually to the weakest point.
- Without a reference number the letter is much weaker. Say so and suggest where to find it — email search, statement, order confirmation — before sending.
- Complaints addresses differ from support addresses and from the address on the shop's website. Point the user at the one on their own paperwork.
- Don't quantify a loss the user can't evidence. "Loss of earnings of 80" needs a document behind it.
- If the company has already made a final offer, the letter becomes a reply to that offer, not a first complaint — ask before assuming.

## Output

- **`complaint-<provider>.md`** — the first letter, ready to copy into email or print, with bracket placeholders for anything the user must fill.
- **`escalation-<provider>.md`** — only when step 6 applies; otherwise not created, and the reply says why.
- **`contact-log.md`** — the dated log table, with the next expected reply as an open row.
- **In chat:** the remedy and the reply deadline in one line, the single next action, the attachments to include, and a plain statement that nothing was sent.
